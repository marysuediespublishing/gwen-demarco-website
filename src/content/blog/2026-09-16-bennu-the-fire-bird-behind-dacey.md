Readers ask me about Dacey more than any other character in Auras & Embers, and the question I get most often is some version of “so she’s a phoenix, right?”

Close. But no. Dacey is a bennu shifter, and the difference matters to me, so let me tell you about the bird.

## The oldest fire bird

Long before the Greeks had their phoenix, the Egyptians had the bennu. It was a sacred bird of Heliopolis, the city of the sun, and it was tied to the sun god Ra and to Atum, the creator. In the oldest versions of the story the bennu is there at the beginning of everything. It flies over the dark waters of creation and comes to rest on the benben, the first mound of earth to rise out of the flood, and its cry breaks the silence and sets time in motion. The obelisks and pyramid caps of Egypt are, in a sense, versions of that first stone the bennu landed on.

The bennu was usually pictured not as an eagle or a songbird but as a tall heron, with along beak and two long plumes trailing from the back of its head. If you have ever seen a great blue heron standing motionless in a Florida marsh, you have seen something very close to how the Egyptians imagined it. Stillness and patience and then sudden, absolute motion.

It was also a bird of renewal. The bennu was linked to the yearly flooding of the Nile, when the river rose and drowned the fields and then withdrew and left them fertile again. Death and return, over and over, on a schedule. Later it was connected to Osiris, the god who died and came back, and to the rising sun that dies every evening and returns every morning.

## Where the phoenix came from

When the Greek historian Herodotus visited Egypt, he wrote down the story of a sacred bird that came to Heliopolis once every five hundred years, and he called it the phoenix. Most scholars think he was describing the bennu, filtered through a Greek imagination. Over the centuries the Greeks and Romans added the parts everyone knows now: the nest that catches fire, the ashes, the new bird rising. The bennu was a creature of sun and flood and first light. The phoenix became a creature of fire and death.

## Why I chose the bennu for Dacey

I wanted Dacey to be fire, but I did not want her to be a metaphor for burning out and starting over. That is the phoenix story, and it is a good one, but it is not hers. The bennu is about endurance. It is the thing that was there at the beginning and will be there at the end, patient as a heron in the reeds and then, when it moves, unstoppable.

That is how I think of Dacey. Her fire is always there, contained under her sigil tattoo, banked but never dormant. Gideon can feel it across a crowded backyard. Most of the time it is a hearth fire, steady and warm. And then, when she needs it, the wings come out in a rush of gold and flame, and everyone who was feeling brave a moment ago remembers they have somewhere else to be.

## A note on the Florida connection

I live in Florida, and herons are everywhere here. Great blues, tricolors, little green herons hunched on dock pilings. Watching one stand utterly still and then strike is a lesson in what patience looks like when it has teeth. I think that is where Dacey really came from, before I ever looked up the mythology.

You will see a great deal more of Dacey’s fire in Book 3. I am not going to say anything else about that yet, except that I enjoyed writing about her and her relationship with Gideon.
